\subsection{Datasets and domain profiles}
\label{sec:datasets}

ReSIDS~v2 claims generality for its core---the event model, the authority rule, the two
Disaster-FD cadences and the reconciliation contract---and not for its detectors.
Detectors trained on one intrusion dataset do not carry over to
another~\cite{dhooge2020interdataset}. In every domain the specialists are retrained,
and only the profile (Section~\ref{sec:api-events}) changes. The evaluation therefore
needs domains that differ in what the core abstracts away, rather than datasets that
maximise accuracy. Three criteria guided the choice:
(i)~the \emph{domain}, i.e., the network the agent protects and the shape of its traffic;
(ii)~the \emph{attributes the core consumes besides the features}, namely whether the
traffic carries time and whether an alarm can be attributed to an emitter; and
(iii)~\emph{verified data integrity}. Label inaccuracy and the base-rate fallacy are
among the most frequent pitfalls of learning-based security
evaluation~\cite{axelsson2000baserate,sommer2010outside,arp2022dos}, so every candidate
was audited before use (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-audit}). Four public datasets are used:
an IEC~61850 substation, an enterprise network, the Modbus control network of a railway
traction substation and a network of consumer IoT devices, the last two only for the
attack categories whose labels the audit could verify. A further profile, without traffic
time or attribution, is exercised by an ablation on the enterprise traffic.
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} summarises them.

Every domain uses two specialists per category of its label set: $N=14$ for ERENO and
CICIDS2017-C, $N=8$ for Electra and $N=6$ for the UNSW IoT traces. Identifiers
(addresses, ports, timestamps, flow and message identities) feed the profile, i.e., the
tick and the attribution, and never the features. This avoids the shortcuts that
identifiers create in benchmark datasets~\cite{flood2024smells}.

\paragraph{ERENO (case study).}
ERENO~\cite{quincozes2023ereno} generates IEC~61850 GOOSE and Sampled Values traffic for
digital substations, with seven attacks against the publish--subscribe messages that
carry protection signals. It is the case study inherited from v1, the only domain whose
traffic is time-critical, and the most demanding of the four. The authors' train/test
split is kept: each periodic GOOSE frame accompanies thousands of SV frames, so a random
re-split would leak. Publisher identities (MAC addresses, \texttt{gocbRef},
\texttt{goID}) and the three absolute-time markers are removed from the features, so
that a specialist learns how an attack behaves and not who performs it or when. The
audit sets two limits. First, the attacks impersonate the single legitimate publisher,
so source attribution would point at the victim; intrusion-driven isolation is
therefore exercised only in synthetic scenarios. Second, the benign volume available at
window level, about twelve minutes, bounds the precision that can be certified at low
prevalence (Section~\ref{sec:api-fp}).

\paragraph{CICIDS2017, improved (attribution and time).}
CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018toward} is the most cited network intrusion benchmark,
but its original labels and flow features contain errors that change detection
results~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting,lanvin2023errors,liu2022error}. The release used
is the improved CICIDS2017 of Liu et al.~\cite{liu2022error}, in the version dated 27 April 2023: a
re-extraction from the original captures that extends the corrected flow meter and
labelling of Engelen et al.~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting}, with five days
(2{,}099{,}976 flows). With the conditions of use below, it is referred to as
CICIDS2017-C. It provides, on captured traffic, three
properties the core needs. Real flow timestamps let the time-based local tick run on live
traffic. Source addresses point at the emitter, either the attacker's gateway or a
compromised internal host, so the \texttt{device\_address} attribution, and therefore
intrusion-driven isolation, can be exercised. A benign-only day (Monday, eight hours)
extends the benign volume available for the prevalence analysis well beyond that of
ERENO. The label set \texttt{cicids2017c-7} groups the classes into DoS, DDoS, port scan,
FTP/SSH brute force, web attack, botnet and infiltration; Heartbleed (11 flows) is
excluded. The audit sets three conditions of use. Flows labelled ``Infiltration --
Portscan'' are merged into port scan, because 91{,}253 of the scan flows are identical
under both labels. The initial TCP window sizes are excluded from the features, because
they fingerprint the scanning host. And the 11{,}979 flows marked \emph{Attempted}, whose
attacks delivered no payload, are labelled benign. The web and infiltration categories
keep only 104 and 36 flows, and their figures are reported with confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Electra Modbus (industrial traffic).}
Electra~\cite{perales2019electra} records the control network of an electric traction
substation built after one in service on a high-speed railway: five programmable logic
controllers and a SCADA system that communicate over Modbus/TCP and S7Comm. The Modbus
release is used, with 16{,}289{,}277 packets over about fourteen hours. It adds
industrial control traffic with request--response polling, and it is the dataset closest
to operational prevalence: the attacks retained below make up 0.41\% of the packets once
the excluded types are removed. Packet timestamps drive the time-based tick, and every
attack packet has the man-in-the-middle host as its source or destination, identified by
its MAC address, so \texttt{device\_address} attribution is exercised in a second domain.
The audit sets one condition of use. Three of the seven attack labels mark the path of a
packet rather than its content: packets relayed unchanged by the man-in-the-middle host
(\texttt{MITM\_UNALTERED}), replayed packets and most read requests issued by that host
have the same content as normal packets and are told apart only by the host's MAC
address. Those three types are excluded. The label set \texttt{electra-4} keeps the four
types visible in the packet content: reconnaissance, unauthorised writes, forced errors
and modified responses. Features are the content fields of each packet (request flag,
function code, error code, register address and value); addresses and time feed the
profile only. The forced-error category keeps 1{,}129 packets, and its figures are
reported with confidence intervals.

\paragraph{UNSW IoT attack traces (IoT devices).}
The UNSW IoT attack traces~\cite{hamza2019unsw} follow ten consumer IoT devices, cameras,
smart plugs and sensors, for 32 to 49 days each, the setting Disaster-FD is designed for.
Each sample describes one device during one minute, by the packet and byte counters of the
flows of its MUD profile, summed per direction, reach and protocol into 41 features. The
234 annotated attacks, of ten minutes each, are interleaved with the devices' normal
traffic and make up 0.40\% of the device-minutes, a realistic prevalence. One agent watches
one device, and the minute is its local tick. The counters belong to the victim device,
not to the emitter, so attribution is \texttt{none}: this domain exercises the core with
time but without an emitter to isolate. The audit sets one condition of use. In every
annotated interval of ARP spoofing, ping of death and Smurf attacks, the counters of the
protocol the attack uses rise above the hour before, but in TCP and UDP floods and
reflections they do so in only 58 to 86\% of the intervals: at one-minute resolution,
those attacks are within the normal variation of a busy device, and specialists trained on
them flag 5.4\% of the normal device-minutes. The label set \texttt{unsw-iot-3} keeps the
three categories visible in every interval; this criterion was set after that first run.
Attack intervals are kept whole across the time-ordered split. The test holds 180 attack
minutes, and the figures are reported with confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Profile without time or attribution (ablation).}
Some releases carry neither timestamps nor addresses; CICIoT2023, rejected by the audit,
is one. Their profile has a count-based local tick, one tick every $M$ samples in arrival
order, and attribution \texttt{none}, under which an intrusion raises alarms but never an
isolation. Rather than a fourth dataset, which would change the data and the profile at
once, this profile is exercised by an ablation: CICIDS2017-C is run under it
(\texttt{it-flow-blind}) with the same specialists and the same split, so that only the
profile changes. Per-flow detection is identical by construction, since the features do
not change. The window triage and the alarms differ: count windows replace time windows,
and alarms carry no emitter.

\paragraph{Datasets not used.}
The original CICIDS2017 release is not used, because of the label errors documented
above; neither is CSE-CIC-IDS2018, which shares its generation methodology and its error
classes~\cite{liu2022error}. NSL-KDD~\cite{tavallaee2009nslkdd}, still common in recent
work, is not considered: its records carry neither timestamps nor addresses, and it
inherits the artefacts of the 1998 DARPA traffic~\cite{mchugh2000darpa}.
CICIoT2023~\cite{neto2023ciciot2023}, first selected as the IoT domain, was rejected: in
four of its seven categories (reconnaissance, web, brute force and spoofing) the labels
mark the capture session rather than the attack, and specialists trained on them flag
benign traffic as attack. Its three volumetric categories have consistent labels, but
they leave out the attacks that matter most in IoT networks. The IoT and industrial IoT
datasets audited to replace it were rejected as well.
ToN\_IoT~\cite{moustafa2021toniot} has consistent labels but a narrow normal class: under
the same protocol, 18\% of its normal flows are flagged. Re-extractions of the same
captures inherit these defects and are not considered either:
CIC-ToN-IoT~\cite{cic2021toniot} re-extracts the ToN\_IoT captures with CICFlowMeter, and
NF-UQ-NIDS~\cite{sarhan2021netflow} merges NetFlow versions of four releases, ToN\_IoT and
CSE-CIC-IDS2018 among them, so that the network a flow comes from also identifies its
source dataset. In IoT-23~\cite{garcia2020iot23}, the ``benign'' flows of the malware
captures are mostly the infected host's unlabelled scanning, and in
X-IIoTID~\cite{alhawawreh2022xiiotid} the classes are recorded in separate sessions and
13\% of the normal connections are flagged. In Kitsune~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}, each
attack lies in its own capture, the features carry the capture time, and normal packets
identify the capture they come from, so that specialists flag up to 92\% of the normal
traffic of one capture. Thirteen further candidates, from industrial,
medical, aerial, power-system, gas-pipeline, water and industrial-control settings, failed
the audit or added no property the four retained datasets lack
(Section~\ref{sec:dataset-audit}). One of them, HAI, has sound labels and genuine process
data but is built for one-class anomaly detection, as are SWaT and
WADI~\cite{mathur2016swat,ahmed2017wadi}, available on request; using them is left to a
one-class variant of the specialists.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
CICIDS2017-C is a laboratory capture, Electra a testbed reproducing an
industrial installation, the UNSW IoT traces a deployment of real consumer devices in a
laboratory, and ERENO synthetic traffic generated by deterministic attack rules. A detector
evaluated on them may not reflect operational
conditions~\cite{sommer2010outside,arp2022dos}. Two label sets are restricted. The four
Electra categories are separable by packet content, so that domain tests the architecture
under node loss rather than the difficulty of detection; their selection follows the label
check of the audit (the path of each packet), made before the evaluation protocol was run
on Electra. The three UNSW IoT categories follow a check of the counters against each
annotated interval, which does not depend on detector output but was applied after a first
run with all seven categories; the excluded floods are real attacks that one-minute
counters cannot separate from normal variation, not label errors. Categories with fewer than about a hundred positive flows yield
per-category figures with wide confidence intervals; they are reported, but no conclusion
is drawn from them. Results are not compared across datasets: the claim under test is
that the same core runs unchanged under different profiles, not that a model trained in
one domain detects attacks in another.
