\begin{abstract}
The evolution of networks has increased their complexity and driven a shift toward
distributed cyber and cyber-physical systems, from enterprise networks to the Internet of
Things and critical infrastructure. While this shift opens new landscapes and opportunities, it also
enlarges the attack surface, calling for adaptive distributed intrusion detection that
keeps working while its own nodes fail. Federated learning (FL) is widely adopted
to train intrusion detectors through a server; gossip learning (GL), far less explored,
lets peers exchange models directly. This research presents ReSIDS, an adaptive intrusion detection system that combines both: it fuses
per-attack gradient-boosted specialists by k-of-n voting and switches its learning mode
at runtime. A federated failure detector, Disaster-FD, supplies a trust level from which
each node switches by itself: to GL when nodes fail, back to FL once they recover, and to
local learning when it is cut off. Applying it elsewhere requires only retraining the
specialists. Since attacks are rare,
individual alerts are mostly false alarms; ReSIDS therefore counts them per window,
network-wide and per specialist: a slow attack accumulates in its own specialist, whereas
false alarms spread. On public IEC 61850 substation (ERENO), enterprise (improved CICIDS2017), railway
Modbus (Electra) and consumer IoT (UNSW) traffic, detection is unchanged as membership
shrinks to 3 nodes (F1-score 95.98\%, 98.87\%, 99.97\% and 72.7\%), while FL alone loses
whole categories, and windowed alarms flag 99.3\%, 73.5\%, 88.7\% and 71.1\% of attack
windows at false-alarm rates up to 0.17\%. Adaptive switching turns the learning topology
into an operational choice.
\end{abstract}
