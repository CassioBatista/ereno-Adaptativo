\begin{abstract}
The evolution of networks has increased their complexity and driven a shift toward
distributed architectures, from enterprise networks to the Internet of Things and
cyber-physical systems. While this shift opens new landscapes and opportunities, it also
enlarges the attack surface, calling for adaptive distributed intrusion detection that
keeps working while its own nodes fail. Federated learning (FL) has been widely adopted
to train intrusion detectors, coordinating a shared model
through a server; gossip learning (GL), far less explored, lets peers exchange models
directly. This research presents ReSIDS, which combines both: it fuses
per-attack gradient-boosted specialists by k-of-n voting and switches between FL and GL
at runtime. A federated failure detector monitors the nodes and commands each switch:
the system moves to GL when nodes fail and returns to FL once they recover. Applying it
to another network requires only retraining the specialists. Because
attacks are rare in real traffic, individual alerts are dominated by false alarms;
ReSIDS therefore counts them per window, network-wide and per specialist: a slow attack accumulates
in its own specialist, whereas false alarms spread. On three public datasets, IEC 61850
substation traffic (ERENO), enterprise traffic (corrected CICIDS2017) and
railway-substation Modbus traffic (Electra), detection is unchanged as membership shrinks
to 3 nodes (F1-score 95.98\%, 98.87\% and 99.97\%), while FL alone loses whole attack
categories, and windowed alarms flag 99.3\%, 73.5\% and 88.7\% of attack windows at
false-alarm rates up to 0.17\%.
Runtime switching makes the learning topology an operational choice rather than a
design-time commitment.
\end{abstract}
